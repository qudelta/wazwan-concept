Investigate

Missingness can be an engineering clue

Suppose a sensor has many missing values during one operating period. Why?
Equipment downtimeThe system was not operating.
MaintenanceMeasurements were intentionally paused.
Sensor malfunctionThe gap itself signals a data-quality issue.
Unusual conditionsThe process moved outside normal operation, e.g. a storm inflow.
A real clue in this dataset
5of 527 days

fall on a Saturday. Whole days are absent from the calendar because of a sampling schedule, not a fault.

A gap in the calendar and a “?” in a cell are different kinds of missing. Both carry information.
Don’t erase information before understanding it.

Every treatment of missing values has a cost

OptionWhat it does hereReasonable when
Drop incomplete rowswt.dropna()527 → 380 days: 28% of the record gone, including unusual days.Gaps are few and random
Drop a columnwt.drop(columns=…)Loses a variable with many gaps, and everything it could tell us.The variable is not needed or mostly empty
Fill with the medianwt.fillna(wt.median())Quick, but writes “typical” values into what may have been untypical days.Gaps are short and random
Domain-informed fillinterpolate · recomputeInterpolate a slowly varying signal; recompute a performance ratio from its inputs.Process knowledge supports it
Keep a “was missing” flagwt["BOD_missing"]Lets a model use the fact that a value was missing.Missingness may itself mean something
Whatever you choose, write it down: the treatment is part of the model. Learn fill values from training data only.

Correlation ≠ causation · a road-engineering example

Do puddles cause potholes?

Observed pattern

Larger puddles go with deeper potholes.

Tempting conclusion

“The puddle caused the pothole.”

Possible hidden factors

Traffic, cracked asphalt and depressions shape both.

A correlation points us toward a question. It does not establish the mechanism.

Engineering action should target the underlying cause, not merely the visible association.

A memorable coincidence

Mozzarella and civil-engineering Ph.D.s

Two U.S. series that rose together, 2000–2009

Published correlation: r ≈ 0.95.
Schematic: shape only, not the published values
The pattern

Per-capita mozzarella consumption and civil-engineering doctorates awarded moved together for a decade.

The lesson

Strong association can arise by coincidence, or because both follow unrelated upward trends.

Cheese does not cause Ph.D.s.
Use the example to remember the principle, not as evidence of a causal relationship.

Column roles

Not every column should become a feature

Feature

Known at prediction time

Information we will genuinely have.

cement, water, age
Target

What we predict

One column, chosen deliberately.

strength · fault class
Identifier

Names, IDs, dates

Labels for a row; often no predictive meaning.

"D-1/3/90" · specimen no.
Leaked information

Not available in time

Would not exist when the prediction is made.

56-day strength → 28-day
Before using a column, ask
  1. 01What does it represent?
  2. 02When would it be known?
  3. 03Could it reveal the answer indirectly?
“I have a column” does not mean “I should give it to the model.”

The silent killer

Data leakage: excellent tests, disappointing reality

01Information unavailable at prediction time
02Influences training or preprocessing
03Test performance looks excellent
04Real-world performance disappoints
Leak through a column

Predicting 28-day strength at the mix-design stage

…with the 56-day result or an acceptance-test pass/fail as an input. Neither exists when the mix is designed.

Leak through preprocessing

Filling or scaling with the full dataset

Medians or scales computed before the split let the test rows quietly shape training.

split first → fit on train → apply to test
Ask: would this information genuinely be available when I make the prediction?

Why keep a test set at all?

Why not evaluate on the data the model has seen?

Seen data

A model may perform well on examples it has already learned from, even by memorising them.

Like a student scoring full marks on last year’s paper after memorising its answers.

Unseen data

We need an honest estimate of how it may perform on new observations: the next mix, the next plate.

Like a new paper: it shows what was understood, not what was remembered.
In Session 03Fit on trainPredict the test rowsCompare with their true yAn honest error estimate
Training performance ≠ generalisation performance.

An important preview

Scaling: same data, very different numbers

Range of each concrete input, on one axis

Aggregates sit in the hundreds of kg/m³; superplasticizer in the tens; age in days.

Sensitive to scale

Distance- and gradient-based methods: k-nearest neighbours, SVMs, neural networks.

Less sensitive

Tree-based methods: decision trees, random forests (Session 04).

The rule
Fit preprocessing on training data, not on the full dataset.
scaler.fit(X_train)      # learn
scaler.transform(X_test) # apply
